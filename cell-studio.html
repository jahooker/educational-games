Where do the electrons go?

Work the circuit bench. Trace the particles in a zinc–copper cell, then take control of a power supply. Use the stations at your own pace; answers remain in this tab until you reload.

Station 01 / 05

Account for the copper

A zinc strip dipped into blue copper(II) sulfate acquires a copper coating. Some zinc enters the liquid as Zn²⁺. The sulfate ions are spectators.

Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s)

See the reasoning

Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻. Losing electrons is oxidation; both sides of the half-equation have zero net charge.

See the reasoning

Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu. The copper(II) ion is reduced and is the oxidising agent.

Station 02 / 05

Close both paths

The same reaction now takes place in two beakers. Oxidation occurs at the anode and reduction at the cathode. Before running the cell, predict the direction electrons will take.

See the reasoning

Zn supplies electrons at the negative anode. Cu²⁺ takes them up at the positive cathode.

Ready. Test the cell with and without the salt bridge.

See the reasoning

NO₃⁻ migrates towards newly formed Zn²⁺; K⁺ migrates towards the side where Cu²⁺ leaves solution. The bridge carries ions, not electrons.

Discuss: The wire is still connected when the bridge is removed. Why is that not enough?

Station 03 / 05

Move copper between strips

Two copper strips, P and N, share one copper(II) sulfate solution. A power supply drives the reaction. At its negative terminal it delivers electrons; at its positive terminal it removes them. Assume only copper is transferred. Each pulse transfers 0.16 g.

Predict which strip will gain mass. Send a pulse, then reverse the connections and try again.

See the reasoning

Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu. P gains copper and is the cathode. Reduction defines the cathode even though its sign differs from that of the voltaic cathode.

See the reasoning

One Cu²⁺ forms at the anode for every Cu²⁺ removed at the cathode. The changes cancel in the bulk solution.

Station 05 / 05

One electron versus two

Optional. Iron metal enters solution as Fe²⁺ in an iron–silver voltaic cell. Silver ions, Ag⁺, deposit as silver metal. Write both half-equations on paper or the whiteboard.

See the reasoning

Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻; Ag⁺ + e⁻ → Ag. Two silver ions take up the two electrons released.

See the reasoning

0.015 × 2 = 0.030 mol Ag. The coefficients follow from conservation of charge.
