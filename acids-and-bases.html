1. Acids and bases transfer protons

A Brønsted–Lowry acid donates a proton, H⁺. A Brønsted–Lowry base accepts a proton. Follow the H⁺ to see which role each species plays.

Worked example: nitric acid in water

HNO₃(aq) + H₂O(l) → NO₃⁻(aq) + H₃O⁺(aq)

HNO₃ loses H⁺ and becomes NO₃⁻: it is the acid. H₂O gains that H⁺ and becomes H₃O⁺: it is the base. The proton has transferred from one species to another.

A conjugate acid–base pair consists of two species differing by one proton. Here the pairs are HNO₃/NO₃⁻ and H₃O⁺/H₂O. Losing H⁺ lowers the charge by one; gaining H⁺ raises it by one.

In water, H⁺ is hydrated; H₃O⁺ represents the hydrated proton. We usually write [H⁺] in pH calculations. Square brackets mean concentration in mol dm⁻³.

2. Strength and concentration describe different things

Concentration describes the amount of a substance per unit volume. A more concentrated acid solution contains more acid per dm³.

Strength describes how extensively an acid ionises in water. A strong acid ionises essentially completely; a weak acid only partially ionises.

Worked examples: the arrows tell a story

HCl(aq) + H₂O(l) → H₃O⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq)

HCl is a strong acid. For a 0.0500 mol dm⁻³ solution, each HCl supplies one proton, so [H⁺] is approximately 0.0500 mol dm⁻³.

CH₃COOH(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ H₃O⁺(aq) + CH₃COO⁻(aq)

Ethanoic acid is weak. Much of it remains as CH₃COOH molecules, so [H⁺] is less than the stated acid concentration. The reversible arrow represents this partial ionisation.

Dilution changes the amount per unit volume. It does not turn a strong acid into a weak acid: dilute HCl is still a strong acid. Compare [H⁺], rather than the acid’s label alone, to decide which solution is more acidic.

3. pH is a logarithmic concentration scale

A more acidic solution has more hydrogen ions and a lower pH. Each fall of one pH unit represents a tenfold increase in [H⁺]. At 25 °C, neutral water has pH 7; acids have lower pH and alkaline solutions higher pH.

Use the numerical concentration in mol dm⁻³:

pH=−log10[H+][H+]=10−pH

pH has no unit. A concentration must be given with its unit.

Worked example: concentration to pH

For [H⁺] = 4.00×10−3 mol dm⁻³:

pH=−log10(4.00×10−3)=2.40

This lies between 2 and 3: the concentration lies between 10−2 and 10−3 mol dm⁻³.

Worked example: pH to concentration

For a solution with pH 2.70:

[H+]=10−2.70=2.00×10−3 mol dm−3

4. Dilution preserves the amount of acid

Adding water spreads the acid through a larger volume. For the strong monoprotic acids used here, [H⁺] decreases in the same ratio as the acid concentration and pH increases.

𝑛=𝑐𝑉𝑐1𝑉1=𝑐2𝑉2

In 𝑛=𝑐𝑉, use volume in dm³. For the dilution relationship, both volumes can use the same unit; 𝑉2 is the final total volume, not just the water added.

Worked example: adding water to HCl

Dilute 20.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ HCl to a final volume of 100.0 cm³.

The amount of acid is unchanged:

𝑛=0.100×0.0200=0.00200 mol

The same amount now occupies 0.1000 dm³:

𝑐2=0.002000.1000=0.0200 mol dm−3
pH=−log10(0.0200)=1.70

The volume increased fivefold, so the concentration fell fivefold. The pH increased from 1.00 to 1.70, not by a factor of five.

Optional: neutralisation uses up acid and base

A base can react with an acid by accepting protons. In the reaction between aqueous HCl and NaOH, hydroxide ions react with hydrogen ions to form water:

H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l)

The full equation is:

HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l)

Na⁺ and Cl⁻ remain in solution as spectator ions. For these reactants, one mole of HCl reacts with one mole of NaOH.

Worked example: how much base is needed?

Find the volume of 0.0500 mol dm⁻³ NaOH needed to neutralise 10.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ HCl.

𝑛(HCl)=0.100×0.0100=0.00100 mol

The equation requires 0.00100 mol of NaOH:

𝑉(NaOH)=𝑛𝑐=0.001000.0500=0.0200 dm3=20.0 cm3

For this strong-acid/strong-base pair at 25 °C, exact neutralisation gives approximately pH 7. The volumes need not be equal: the reacting amounts must match the equation.
