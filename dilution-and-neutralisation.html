1. Adding water changes concentration

Amount, 𝑛, tells us how many moles are present. Concentration, 𝑐, tells us the amount per unit volume. Adding water spreads the same amount of acid through a larger volume.

𝑛=𝑐𝑉𝑐=𝑛𝑉

With 𝑐 in mol dm⁻³, use 𝑉 in dm³: divide a volume in cm³ by 1000. For dilution alone, 𝑐1𝑉1=𝑐2𝑉2 because the amount is unchanged; 𝑉2 is the final total volume.

Worked example: dilute hydrochloric acid

Dilute 20.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ HCl to 100.0 cm³. HCl ionises completely, so each mole of acid supplies one mole of H⁺. Neglect water’s contribution here.

𝑛=0.100×0.0200=0.00200 mol
[H+]=0.002000.1000=0.0200 mol dm−3
pH=−log10(0.0200)=1.70

The concentration falls fivefold. pH rises from 1.00 to 1.70 because pH is logarithmic. A tenfold dilution would raise pH by one unit for these strong-acid solutions.

2. Neutralisation changes the amount of acid

In a reaction between a strong acid and an aqueous metal hydroxide, H⁺ and OH⁻ react to form water. The net ionic equation shows the species that change:

H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l)

For hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide, the full equation is

HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l)

Na⁺ and Cl⁻ are spectator ions: they remain dissolved and unchanged. NaCl is the salt formed; in solution its ions remain separate.

The equivalence point is reached when the acid and base have reacted in the amounts required by the balanced equation. Here one mole of HCl reacts with one mole of NaOH.

Worked example: how much base is needed?

Find the volume of 0.0500 mol dm⁻³ NaOH needed to neutralise 10.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ HCl.

𝑛(HCl)=0.100×0.0100=0.00100 mol

The 1:1 ratio requires 0.00100 mol NaOH:

𝑉(NaOH)=0.001000.0500=0.0200 dm3=20.0 cm3.

The base is half as concentrated, so twice its volume supplies the required amount. At equivalence, this strong-acid/strong-base mixture has approximately pH 7 at 25 °C.

3. The remaining acid determines the pH

When base is added to excess strong acid, the base reacts completely. Some hydrogen ions remain. Their concentration depends on both the amount left and the total volume of the mixture.

For a mixture with strong monoprotic acid left over:

[H+]=amount of H⁺ remainingtotal solution volume

Use mol and dm³, then pH=−log10[H+].

Worked example: not enough base to neutralise

Mix 20.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ HCl with 10.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ NaOH. Assume additive volumes and complete reaction.

𝑛(H⁺ initially)=0.100×0.0200=0.00200 mol
𝑛(OH⁻ added)=0.100×0.0100=0.00100 mol

Each OH⁻ removes one H⁺, leaving

𝑛(H⁺ remaining)=0.00200−0.00100=0.00100 mol.

These ions occupy the combined 30.0 cm³, or 0.0300 dm³:

[H+]=0.001000.0300=0.0333 mol dm−3
pH=−log10(0.001000.0300)=1.48.

The mixture is still acidic. If only water were added, no H⁺ would be consumed; the lower concentration would be due to the larger volume alone.

Optional: the equation sets the mole ratio

A mole of a dissolved metal hydroxide does not always supply one mole of OH⁻. For example, each formula unit of Ba(OH)₂ provides two OH⁻ ions:

Ba(OH)₂(aq) → Ba²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq)

Its neutralisation equation is

Ba(OH)₂(aq) + 2HCl(aq) → BaCl₂(aq) + 2H₂O(l)

One mole of Ba(OH)₂ reacts with two moles of HCl. The ionic reaction still uses one H⁺ for each OH⁻.

Worked example: two hydroxide ions per formula unit

Find the volume of 0.0500 mol dm⁻³ HCl needed to neutralise 15.0 cm³ of 0.0200 mol dm⁻³ Ba(OH)₂.

𝑛(Ba(OH)₂)=0.0200×0.0150=0.000300 mol
𝑛(OH⁻)=2×0.000300=0.000600 mol

The acid must supply the same amount of H⁺:

𝑉(HCl)=0.0006000.0500=0.0120 dm3=12.0 cm3.

Concentrations describe formula units of the stated solute. Use its formula and the balanced equation to connect this to the reacting ions.
